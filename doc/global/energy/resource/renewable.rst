.. _renewable:

Non-Biomass Renewable Resources
================================

:numref:`tab-depl` shows the assumed global potentials of non-biomass renewable energy deployment,
by resource type, in |name|.
Wind and solar potentials follow the assessment made for the ScenarioMIP-CMIP7 quantification
(Fricko et al., 2026 :cite:`fricko_wu_2026`).
On- and offshore wind potentials draw on the assessment of the U.S. National Renewable Energy Laboratory
(Eurek et al., 2017 :cite:`eurek_wind_2017`; Sullivan, 2014 :cite:`sullivan_wind_2014`),
utility-scale solar PV potentials on an in-house GIS-based assessment,
and rooftop solar PV potentials on the rooftop-area dataset of Joshi et al. (2024 :cite:`joshi_rooftop_2024`).
Hydro and concentrating solar power potentials are based on
the assessment described in the Global Energy Assessment
(Rogner et al., 2012 :cite:`rogner_chapter_2012`) and Johnson et al. (2016 :cite:`johnson_vre_2016`)
and are the same across all narratives.
Geothermal deployment is not limited by a resource potential in |name|
but by the technology diffusion constraints described in Section :ref:`techchange`.
In this context, it is important to note that typical MESSAGE
scenarios do not consider the full technical potential of renewable energy resources, but rather only a subset of those potentials, owing to additional constraints (e.g., sustainability
criteria, technology diffusion and systems integration issues, and other economic considerations). These constraints may lead to a significant reduction of the technical potential.

*Notes: MESSAGE renewable energy potentials are estimated based on the methods explained in* Pietzcker et al., 2014 :cite:`pietzcker_solar_2014`, Eurek et al., 2017 :cite:`eurek_wind_2017`,
Joshi et al., 2024 :cite:`joshi_rooftop_2024`, Christiansson, 1995 :cite:`christiansson_diffusion_1995`, *and* Rogner et al., 2012 :cite:`rogner_chapter_2012`. *The potentials for non-combustible renewable energy sources are specified
in terms of the electricity or heat that can be produced by specific technologies (i.e., from a secondary energy perspective). By contrast, the technical potentials from* :cite:`rogner_chapter_2012`
*refer to the flows of energy that could become available as inputs for technology conversion. So for example, the technical potential for wind is given as the kinetic energy available for wind power
generation, whereas the deployment potential would only be the electricity that could be generated by the wind turbines.*

On- and offshore wind potentials are derived from data of the U.S. National Renewable Energy Laboratory
(Eurek et al., 2017 :cite:`eurek_wind_2017`; Sullivan, 2014 :cite:`sullivan_wind_2014`).
Full deployment distance-based potentials are used,
dropping sites with capacity factors below 26% onshore and 24% offshore,
as these are economically infeasible and result in too high electricity generation costs
(Johnson et al., 2016 :cite:`johnson_vre_2016`).
The absolute annual potential remains constant over time and is the same across the narratives,
with the pace of technological learning the main determinant
of the associated electricity generation costs and therefore of deployment.

Utility-scale solar PV potentials rely on an in-house GIS-based assessment
following a well-established geospatial bottom-up methodology.
Geographical exclusion zones where deployment is unfavourable or impractical,
such as water bodies, elevated areas and protected areas,
are combined into a mask applied to the global land area.
The land-based exclusion masks are based on the global assessment of the World Bank
(Suri et al., 2020 :cite:`suri_pv_2020`),
and the open-water masks on the surface water product of the EU Joint Research Centre
(Pekel et al., 2016 :cite:`pekel_water_2016`).
The remaining suitable land is classified into three land types by suitability for solar PV
using the Copernicus land cover dataset (Buchhorn et al., 2020 :cite:`buchhorn_copernicus_2020`).
The first layer, L1, covers desert, arid and sparsely vegetated zones,
the largest area globally at 41 million km\ :sup:`2`.
L2 covers forest areas with a tree canopy below 30%, 9 million km\ :sup:`2`,
and L3 largely consists of cropland, 14 million km\ :sup:`2`.
The suitable land is divided into hexagons of 10 km\ :sup:`2`,
each assigned its distance from the nearest urban conglomeration
and aggregated into buffer zones of 10 km, 20 km, 100 km and beyond per country and region.
This allows deployment to be prioritised by distance from demand centres and by land type.
Combined with the average capacity factor of each hexagon from Solargis,
a ground coverage ratio of 50% and a panel efficiency of 15%,
regional solar deployment potentials are derived.

Rooftop solar PV potentials build on the rooftop-area dataset of Joshi et al. (2024 :cite:`joshi_rooftop_2024`),
which provides the available rooftop area and its average capacity factor on a 10 km\ :sup:`2` grid.
The global average net rooftop area available for deployment is assumed to be
30% in SSP5, 35% in SSP2 and 40% in SSP1, at a panel efficiency of 15%.
Whereas the utility-scale assessment holds the 2020 land cover and the panel efficiency constant,
the rooftop potential takes its 2050 maximum as the upper bound from 2020 to 2100,
thereby incorporating changes in future urban morphology.
These assumptions provide a conservative estimate of solar PV deployment potentials
with a uniform assumption uncertainty across the scenarios,
aligned with the assumptions for wind.
Unlike wind, the solar potentials differ across the narratives.
The utility-scale potential ranges from about 5000 EJ/yr in SSP3 to about 10700 EJ/yr in SSP1,
and the rooftop potential from 64 EJ/yr in SSP1 to 82 EJ/yr in SSP5,
the latter because slower population growth in SSP1 means less new housing
and hence less rooftop area.

The variability of solar and wind is represented through integration constraints
on curtailment, firm capacity and flexibility,
following the reduced-form approach of Johnson et al. (2016 :cite:`johnson_vre_2016`).
They are differentiated across the narratives,
easing the integration of variable renewables in SSP1 and LED
and tightening it in SSP3, SSP4 and SSP5,
as described in Section :ref:`syst_integration`.
The resulting cost-availability curves by narrative are shown in :numref:`fig-vre-cost`.

classified by region and capacity factor for solar multiples (SM) of 1 and 3 (:numref:`tab-csp`).
They are the same across all narratives.
